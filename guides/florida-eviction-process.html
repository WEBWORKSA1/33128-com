The Florida Eviction Process, Step by Step (for Tenants and Landlords)

How residential evictions work in Miami-Dade: notices, the 5-day answer deadline, depositing rent into the court registry, the writ of possession and where to find help.

By the 33128.com editorial team · Updated November 2026 · 8 min read

Roughly 97% of homes in ZIP 33128 are renter-occupied, which makes understanding Florida's eviction process essential for residents and property owners alike. Florida's process is one of the fastest in the country — a tenant who misses a deadline can lose possession in a matter of weeks.

1. The notice

Most evictions begin with a written notice. For unpaid rent, a landlord typically serves a 3-day notice (excluding weekends and legal holidays) demanding payment or possession. For other lease violations, a 7-day notice is common — some allow the tenant to cure, others don't. Month-to-month tenancies are ended with a longer notice. Self-help evictions — changing locks, shutting off utilities or removing belongings — are illegal in Florida.

2. The complaint and summons

If the issue isn't resolved, the landlord files an eviction complaint in County Court. Once served, the tenant generally has 5 days (excluding weekends and legal holidays) to respond to the possession claim.

3. The court registry rule — the critical step for tenants

In a nonpayment case, a tenant who wants to raise any defense other than payment must generally deposit the alleged rent owed into the court registry (or file a motion asking the court to decide the amount) by the answer deadline, and keep depositing rent as it comes due. Failing to do so can result in an immediate judgment for the landlord without a hearing. This is the single most important rule tenants miss.

4. Judgment and writ of possession

If the landlord wins, the clerk issues a writ of possession. The sheriff posts it, and the tenant has 24 hours before the sheriff can return to put the landlord back in possession.

Tenants: what to do now

  • Calendar the deadline the day you're served — use our deadline calculator with "business days."
  • Contact legal aid or a tenant attorney immediately; free help may be available depending on income.
  • Keep records of every payment, repair request and photo of conditions.

Landlords: avoid costly mistakes

  • Use the exact statutory notice language and count days correctly — a defective notice can force you to start over.
  • Don't accept partial rent after a notice without understanding how it affects your case.
  • Consider an attorney for contested cases; fee-shifting provisions can make it affordable.
